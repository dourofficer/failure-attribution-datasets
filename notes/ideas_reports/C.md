# Batch C report — OOD detection, distribution shift, robust adaptation

Reader note: all 8 papers read end to end (method, experiments, appendices). Page numbers refer to the PDF-page numbers printed in the text extraction. Ideas are mapped to the target problem using the brief's vocabulary (when/who/what, L0–L2, text-only vs grey-box, proposals 1 and 2).

Recurring structural fact exploited below: **successful trajectories are (nearly) pure "good step" samples; failed trajectories are a mixture of good and bad steps.** Four of the eight papers (OOD-with-human-feedback, Feature Resonance, UDA-with-missing-subpopulation, and the survey's AD framing) give concrete machinery for exactly this "clean positives + wild mixture" situation.

## 1. Generalized OOD Detection and Beyond in the VLM Era: A Survey (Miyai et al., TMLR 07/2025, arXiv 2407.21794v2)

**Paper.** A survey, not a method. It (i) re-draws the "generalized OOD detection" taxonomy for the CLIP era along four axes — covariate vs semantic shift, single- vs multi-class normality, whether ID classification is required, inductive vs transductive (Fig. 1, Sec. 2.1, p.5–6) — and argues that semantic AD/ND, OSR and outlier detection have gone dormant while sensory anomaly detection (AD) and OOD detection remain active (Table 1, Sec. 2.3, p.7–8); (ii) catalogues VLM-based OOD scores: post-hoc softmax-over-text-similarity (MCM), retrieved/generated negative labels (NegLabel, EOE), learned negative prompts (LSN, NegPrompt), and few-shot training-free memory banks of normal references (WinCLIP+, InCTRL) (Table 2, Sec. 4–5, p.11–18); (iii) runs its own comparison (Table 3, p.21): on ImageNet far-OOD, AUROC is 90–94 for all methods; on near-OOD (SSB-Hard/NINCO) the best is 80.9 (CLIPN) and most are 67–73; ImageNet-20 is saturated (>97); method rankings flip across benchmarks; (iv) describes the LVLM-era successor of OOD detection, "Unsolvable Problem Detection" (UPD: absent-answer, incompatible-answer-set, incompatible-visual-question), with the finding that "most LVLMs hardly hesitate to answer" and a "Dual Accuracy" metric that counts a case correct only if the solvable and the unsolvable twin are both handled (Sec. 7.2, p.23–24). The one methodological point that matters for us is the AD-vs-OOD distinction in Sec. 5.4.1 (p.19): in AD "the anomaly space is limited … This limited anomaly space allows even simple prompts to achieve decent performance. Therefore … all AD methods utilize anomaly prompts. Conversely, in OOD detection … anything semantically different from the ID class is considered OOD. Thus, utilizing naive manual OOD prompts is forbidden."

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 1.1 — Frame step-level attribution as sensory anomaly detection with "anomaly prompts" = failure taxonomy.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Sec. 5.4.1 (p.19) and Sec. 5.1.1 (p.16): AD scores use paired prompts ("normal [class]" vs "anomalous [class]"); CLIP-AC with both prompts beats normal-only prompts; WinCLIP ensembles many normal/anomaly templates; InCTRL (Sec. 5.2.3, p.18) "trains a model to discriminate anomalies from normal samples by learning to identify residuals or discrepancies between query images and a set of few-shot normal images … from auxiliary data", then at test time compares the query against a few normal references from the *target* dataset.
- *Mapping:* the "sample" is a step s_t (agent output + observation) embedded by a text encoder or the agent LLM's hidden state; "normal" = steps of successful trajectories of the same task family/role (a memory bank, 250 clean runs in AgentHallu, 1,284 in HORIZON, 610 PASS in Failure-as-a-Process); "anomaly prompts" = the 9–20 taxonomy modes phrased as step-level descriptions ("the agent calls a tool with arguments inconsistent with the previous observation", …). Score(s_t) = residual against the k nearest normal references (InCTRL style) combined with max similarity to anomaly prompts minus similarity to normal prompts (CLIP-AC style). Output: ranked candidate steps (nDCG), "when" as the first step above threshold, "what" as the argmax anomaly prompt. Supervision L1 (needs outcome labels to define the normal bank); text-only. Cross-framework transfer is the InCTRL recipe: train the residual discriminator on frameworks A, B; at test time on framework C supply only a handful of C's successful runs as references.
- *Datasets / baseline:* fit on AgentHallu clean + HORIZON successes; evaluate step ranking on AgentHallu hallucinated (443), Who&When, TrajErrBench; baseline = zero-shot LLM judge per step, OAT one-class model, plain kNN distance to the normal bank (no learned residual).
- *Relation to proposals:* strengthens proposal 2 (gives a label-free per-step feature and a cross-domain few-shot variant that OAT lacks); orthogonal to proposal 1.
- *Main risk:* in agent traces the "normal" manifold is highly multi-modal (many tools/roles/tasks); a nearest-normal residual may fire on rare-but-correct steps (covariate novelty) rather than errors — exactly the far/near-OOD confusion the survey documents (near-OOD AUROC ≈ 70–80 vs far-OOD ≈ 90–94).
- *Plausibility 3, Effort M.*

**Card 1.2 — "Dual accuracy" and false-alarm rate on clean runs as mandatory metrics for attributors.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* UPD evaluation (Sec. 7.2.1, p.24): Standard Accuracy on solvable problems, UPD Accuracy on unsolvable ones, and Dual Accuracy that requires both members of a (solvable, unsolvable) pair to be right; Finding 1 (p.24): LLaVA-1.5 and CogVLM "completely fail to withhold answering".
- *Mapping:* every attribution benchmark that has successes (AgentHallu 250 clean, HORIZON 1,284, Failure-as-a-Process 610 PASS, TRAIL) should be evaluated with (a) step accuracy on failures, (b) abstention/"no error" accuracy on successes (an attributor asked "which step is wrong?" on a clean run must say none), (c) the paired dual accuracy where a task has both a failed and a successful run. The UPD finding predicts that prompted LLM attributors will almost never abstain, so published step-accuracy numbers on failure-only datasets overstate usefulness as online monitors; the calibration of P(fail | prefix) in the brief needs exactly these negatives. Also test two UPD prompt variants (option "none of the above" vs instruction) since the paper found effectiveness is model-dependent. Supervision L1; text-only.
- *Datasets / baseline:* AgentHallu (natural paired structure: hallucinated vs clean runs of the same tasks), Failure-as-a-Process; baseline = current prompted judges without a "none" option.
- *Relation:* new as an evaluation protocol; directly supports proposal 2's calibration claims (a hazard model trained on both outcomes gets abstention for free, prompted judges do not).
- *Main risk:* few datasets have paired success/failure runs of the same task instance; dual accuracy may have to be computed at task-family level.
- *Plausibility 4, Effort S.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "the ranking of methods differs significantly depending on the dataset … LoCoOp outperforms CoOp on the standard ImageNet far OOD benchmark, but underperforms on the near OOD benchmarks" (Sec. 6.4, p.22); Table 3 (p.21): MCM AUROC 90.66 far-OOD vs 68.91 near-OOD.
2. "The results on ImageNet near OOD and ImageNet-X show that the methods with the highest score reach only around 80% accuracy" (Sec. 6.4, p.22).
3. "Most LVLMs, especially open-source LVLMs, have significantly low UPD accuracies … LLaVA-1.5 and CogVLM … completely fail to withhold answering" (Sec. 7.2.2, p.24).
4. AD vs OOD prompt rule, Sec. 5.4.1 (p.19), quoted above.
5. Only 3 top-venue VLM papers on semantic AD/ND 2021–Apr 2025 vs ~27 on OOD detection (Table 1, p.4).

**Code/data released?** Curated paper list at github.com/AtsuMiyai/Awesome-OOD-VLM; no new code or data.

## 2. Out-of-Distribution Learning with Human Feedback (Bai, Du, Rainey, Parameswaran, Li; arXiv 2408.07772, Aug 2024)

**Paper.** Setting (Sec. 2, p.2–3): labeled ID set S_in plus an unlabeled "wild" set drawn from (1−π_c−π_s)P_in + π_c P_cov + π_s P_sem (π_c = 0.5, π_s = 0.1 in experiments). Method (Sec. 3.1, p.3–5): train an ID classifier on S_in; compute the reference gradient ∇̄ = mean over S_in of ∇ℓ (Eq. 3); for each wild point compute ∇ℓ(f(x̃), ŷ_x̃) − ∇̄ with its *predicted* label (Eq. 4); take the top singular vector v of that gradient matrix; score τ_i = ⟨∇ℓ_i − ∇̄, v⟩² (Eq. 5). Select k wild points for human labels (label from Y ∪ {⊥}, ⊥ = semantic OOD) by top-k, near-boundary (around the 95%-ID threshold), or mixed (Fig. 2, p.5). Then jointly train (Eq. 7, p.5) the multi-class classifier on S_in ∪ labelled-covariate points and a binary OOD detector g_θ on S_in vs labelled-semantic points with a sigmoid surrogate of the 0/1 level-set loss, α = 10; one round of feedback suffices (p.6). Theorem 1 (p.5): generalization error on covariate-OOD is bounded by a term proportional to the gradient discrepancy ‖∇R_{S_in} − ∇R_{S_selected}‖ plus O(√(d log m_c / m_c)). Results (Table 1, p.7; WRN-40-2, CIFAR-10 ID, CIFAR-10-C covariate, k = 1000): OOD acc 90.31 / FPR95 4.91 with Texture as semantic OOD vs SCONE 85.56 / 37.15; SVHN FPR95 0.12. Budget ablation (Table 3, p.8): k = 100/500/1000/2000 → FPR95 22.16/7.50/4.91/3.60. Score ablation (Table 4, p.8): gradient 4.91 vs margin 6.37, entropy 8.19, BADGE 8.77, random 9.45, energy 10.89. Strategy ablation (Table 5, p.9, k = 100): top-k picks 0 ID / 57 covariate / 43 semantic; near-boundary picks 44 / 50 / 6 and is worst. PACS average 91.3 vs best DG baseline 86.9 (Table 2, p.9).

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 2.1 — Gradient-projection acquisition function: which steps to ask a human (L2) or a rollout (L1+) about.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Eqs. 3–6 (p.3–4) and Table 4–5 (p.8–9). The score is the squared projection of a wild point's *pseudo-labelled* gradient (centred by the ID reference gradient) onto the principal direction of all wild gradients; this direction "indicates the direction of OOD data in the wild" (Fig. 1 caption, p.4). Top-k selection returned zero pure-ID points among 100 and beat uncertainty sampling by a wide margin for the detector (FPR 22 vs 42).
- *Mapping:* S_in = steps from successful trajectories (clean positives; AgentHallu 250 runs, HORIZON 1,284, Failure-as-a-Process 610 PASS, TRAIL). Wild = all steps of failed trajectories (mixture of good steps, benign-unusual steps, and the few error steps). Train a small step-level model on S_in with a pretext loss that has a gradient per step — e.g., a linear/MLP head on a frozen embedding (text-only) or on the agent model's last-token hidden state of the step (grey-box) predicting the step's action type / tool / role (a cheap multi-class pretext that exists for every framework), or a step-is-from-success classifier. Compute τ for every failed-trajectory step; the top-k across the corpus are the steps to send to annotators (L2) or to the perturbation screen of proposal 1 (L1+ rollouts at those positions only). Output: a ranked candidate list per dataset (nDCG) and an acquisition policy. Three-way label Y ∪ {⊥} becomes {benign-unusual step (relabel as good, add to S_in), erroneous step (train the binary detector)}.
- *Datasets / baseline:* evaluate how often top-k by τ hits the human-labelled decisive step in AgentHallu / Failure-as-a-Process / Who&When (recall@k of t*); baselines = random steps, LLM-judge confidence ranking, entropy/margin of the same head, SAL (Du et al. 2024, the same gradient score without the human loop), and last-k-steps heuristic (errors cluster near the end in injected sets).
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 1 (the perturbation screen is O(T·N) rollouts per trajectory; τ gives an acquisition function to spend rollouts on ≪T positions) and proposal 2 (a few hundred labelled steps turn the L1 hazard fit into a semi-supervised one; Table 3 says returns saturate around k ≈ 1000 labels corpus-wide).
- *Main risk:* in this paper π_s = 0.1 and the semantic-OOD points are a different *dataset*; in a failed trajectory the error fraction is ~1/T and error steps are textually near-identical to good steps, so the top singular direction may align with task/tool covariate variation (Table 5 shows top-k already captures 57% covariate points). The pretext loss choice is load-bearing and untested.
- *Plausibility 4, Effort M.*

**Card 2.2 — Covariate-vs-semantic relabelling to make an attributor transfer across frameworks.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* the annotation protocol (Sec. 3.1, p.5): the human returns a class label for covariate-shifted points and ⊥ for semantic-shifted ones; the two sets feed different loss terms (Eq. 7–8, p.5); Theorem 1 + Appendix L (p.31–32) show the achievable OOD accuracy tracks the gradient discrepancy between S_in and the selected covariate points (Table 11: 0.00030–0.00040).
- *Mapping:* when a step detector trained on framework A (e.g., Who&When MAS logs) is applied to framework B (SWE-Bench Pro, τ²-bench in TrajErrBench; CLI coding in Failure-as-a-Process), most of its flags are covariate novelty (new tool names, log formats, longer observations). Run Card 2.1's selection on B's unlabeled steps, have annotators mark each flagged step as "fine, just new" vs "error", retrain with the two-term loss. The gradient-discrepancy statistic between A's steps and B's relabelled-benign steps is a cheap pre-labelling predictor of how large the in-domain/cross-domain gap will be, and of which source dataset to transfer from. Supervision L2 with budget ≈ 100–500 steps per target framework; text-only or grey-box.
- *Datasets / baseline:* source Who&When / Who&When Pro; targets TrajErrBench, AgentErrorBench, Failure-as-a-Process; baseline = direct transfer without relabelling, and relabelling random steps.
- *Relation:* new (adaptation of attributors across benchmarks is not in either proposal).
- *Main risk:* requires training a parametric detector (the current best attributors are prompted LLMs with no gradients); the benefit over simply fine-tuning on the k labelled steps is unproven.
- *Plausibility 3, Effort M.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "we substantially improve the OOD classification accuracy by 5.82% on covariate-shifted CIFAR-10 data, while reducing the average OOD detection error by 15.16% (FPR95)" vs SCONE (Sec. 1, p.2).
2. Table 3 (p.8): FPR95 22.16 / 7.50 / 4.91 / 3.60 for budgets 100 / 500 / 1000 / 2000; "a marginal difference between k = 1000 and k = 2000".
3. Table 4 (p.8): gradient score FPR95 4.91 vs random 9.45, entropy 8.19, BADGE 8.77.
4. Table 5 (p.9): top-k sampling (k = 100) selects 0 ID, 57 covariate-OOD, 43 semantic-OOD; near-boundary selects 44 / 50 / 6 and gives FPR95 41.72.
5. "In practice, we find that using one round of human feedback is sufficient to achieve strong performance." (Sec. 4.1, p.6).

**Code/data released?** No code URL in the text (implementation described as PyTorch 1.8.1/1.13.1); datasets are public CIFAR/PACS variants.

## 3. Harnessing Feature Resonance under Arbitrary Target Alignment for OOD Node Detection (Yang, Zhao, Li, …, Wang; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2502.16076v2)

**Paper.** Label-agnostic OOD detection: known ID nodes X_known (no class labels needed), wild nodes = unlabeled ID + OOD. Train a *single linear layer* h_θ (hidden 16) with MSE to pull every known-ID representation to one arbitrary random target vector e (Eq. 2, p.4). After each epoch t compute, for every wild node, the one-step representation movement Δh_θt(x̃) = h_θt+1(x̃) − h_θt(x̃) and the "resonance score" τ_i = ‖Δh‖₂ (Def. 2, p.4). Unknown ID nodes move more than OOD nodes ("feature resonance"); Eq. 19 (App. G.1, p.28) explains why: Δh = 2αE(x̃ X_knownᵀ)(X_known Wᵀ − 1ᵀe), i.e. the movement of x̃ is proportional to its feature similarity with the known-ID set and the target e is irrelevant. The effect peaks in mid-training (Fig. 2, p.3; Fig. 3, p.24; IB-style account in App. G.2), so the epoch t* is picked with a small binary ID/OOD validation set maximising AUROC (Eq. 3, p.4). RSL then takes the n smallest-τ wild nodes as candidate OOD, generates SGLD synthetic OOD anchored on them (Eq. 4, p.5), and trains a binary energy classifier (Eq. 5). Results (Table 2, p.7, no labels used): τ alone (RSL w/o classifier) beats the SOTA EnergyDef on 4/5 datasets, e.g. WikiCS FPR95 70.38 vs 83.17, Amazon 19.56 vs 32.43; full RSL WikiCS 49.23, Amazon 19.60; avg FPR95 reduction 9.70% (τ alone) and 15.20% (RSL). Table 6 (p.8): aligning to one random vector works as well as or better than aligning to class-wise ETF targets with true labels (WikiCS AUROC 79.15 vs 71.03). Table 7 (p.9): τ beats cosine/Euclidean/Mahalanobis-to-prototype and EnergyDef for picking candidate OOD. Fig. 4–5 (p.25): the single-step score beats cumulative trajectory norm and sliding windows, and performance degrades monotonically with window width. Generality (App. F.7, Table 14–15, p.27): on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 features, τ gives avg FPR95 21.28 vs KNN 24.47 (CE) and 6.88 vs KNN+ 8.09 (SupCon); ImageNet-R FPR 15.27 vs KNN+ 30.20. Table 4 (p.8): better-initialised ID representations (more supervised-contrastive pretraining) → stronger resonance. Time: τ alone uses 79.81% less time than EnergyDef (Table 11, p.26). Limitation acknowledged: needs the validation set to pick t.

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 3.1 — Resonance score for label-free ranking of candidate error steps.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Def. 2 and Eq. 3 (p.4), Eq. 19 (p.28), Tables 2, 6, 7, 14. The whole detector is one linear layer trained for a few epochs with MSE to a random vector; the score is the per-sample L2 movement of the representation in one update at the resonance epoch.
- *Mapping:* "known ID" = embeddings of steps from successful trajectories (L1 outcome labels only; no step labels, no failure taxonomy). "Wild" = steps of failed trajectories. Representation: frozen text embedding of (action, observation) per step, or — grey-box — the agent model's hidden state at the last token of each step (the paper's Table 4/14 says resonance strength scales with representation quality, so a strong embedding model or mid-layer LLM state is preferred over a small encoder). Train h_θ, compute τ_t for each failed-trajectory step, rank ascending (low movement = does not share the good-step manifold = candidate error). Pick the epoch with a few dozen labelled steps (L2, tiny) or by the mid-training heuristic. Output: ranked candidate list (nDCG) and a decisive step as the earliest step below a quantile threshold; aggregated per agent it gives "who". Because the score needs no labels it can be computed per dataset/framework separately — no cross-domain transfer required at all, which sidesteps the in-domain/cross-domain gap.
- *Datasets / baseline:* AgentHallu (clean vs hallucinated with step labels), Failure-as-a-Process (PASS vs FAIL with t_err/t_lock/t_obs), HORIZON successes + TRAIL; baselines = kNN/Mahalanobis distance to success-step prototypes (Table 7 shows τ wins), OAT one-class CDE, LLM-judge ranking, position prior.
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 2 at the L0/L1 level — a training-dynamics surrogate for h_t that is independent of the outcome likelihood used by the hazard model, so the two can be stacked; positions against OAT (density/one-class) with a different signal.
- *Main risk:* steps of one trajectory share the task context, so τ may primarily encode task-similarity to the success corpus (covariate) rather than correctness; steps *after* the error in a failed run are also off-manifold (propagation), so the ascending ranking may select late symptoms (t_obs) rather than the cause (t_err) — the earliest-below-threshold rule is needed and untested. Resonance was shown on nodes/images, never on sequential text.
- *Plausibility 4, Effort M.*

**Card 3.2 — Anchor synthetic error injection on label-free natural candidates.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Sec. 2.3 (p.5): SGLD synthetic OOD is pulled toward the candidate OOD set via the (1−λ)E_{x∼X_cand}(x − x̂) term in Eq. 4; Fig. 7 (p.25) shows EnergyDef's unanchored synthetic OOD "fail to accurately represent the actual features of OOD nodes … and even overlap with ID nodes", while anchored ones align with real OOD; Table 2 rows "RSL w/o V_syn" vs "RSL" quantify the gain (WikiCS FPR 52.19 → 49.23).
- *Mapping:* the brief notes injected-failure datasets (Who&When Pro, Aegis, TracerTraj, CORRECT-Error) have error positions skewed to the start/end and are stylistically unlike natural failures. Use Card 3.1's low-τ natural candidate steps as anchors: inject errors at the positions and of the kinds that the candidates exhibit (rewrite the candidate step with an LLM into a corrupted variant, or inject at the same relative position in similar successful runs), then train a step classifier on clean + anchored-synthetic. Supervision L1 (+ L0 candidates); text-only. Output: a trained step classifier for when/what.
- *Datasets / baseline:* anchors from AgentHallu/Failure-as-a-Process/HORIZON; evaluate against training on Who&When Pro / CORRECT-Error injections alone; test on natural Who&When, TrajErrBench.
- *Relation:* complementary to proposal 1 (its fragility curves inject at arbitrary positions k; anchoring says where realistic injections should go).
- *Main risk:* LLM-rewritten corruptions may still be detectable by style; the paper's anchored synthesis operates in embedding space, which is not available as text for an LLM judge.
- *Plausibility 3, Effort M.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "the FPR95 metric is reduced by an average of 15.20% compared to the current state-of-the-art methods" (Sec. 1, p.3); τ alone "achieves an average reduction of 9.70%" (Sec. 3.2, p.8).
2. "even when all known ID node representations are aligned toward one single random fixed target vector, the trajectories of unknown ID nodes are still longer than those of unknown OOD nodes" (Sec. 2.1, p.4); Table 6: random vector AUROC 79.15 vs true multi-label 71.03 on WikiCS.
3. "as the sliding window width increases, the detection performance for OOD nodes gradually decreases, which indicates that finer-grained information improves OOD node detection" (App. F.3, p.26).
4. "feature resonance does not persist throughout the entire training process but rather occurs at specific stages of training … in the middle stages" (Sec. 2.2, p.4).
5. Table 14 (p.27): on CIFAR-10 image features, FR τ avg FPR95 21.28/AUROC 95.90 (CE) and 6.88/98.67 (SupCon) vs KNN+ 8.09/98.56; Table 15: ImageNet-R FPR 15.27 vs KNN+ 30.20.

**Code/data released?** Yes: https://github.com/ShenzhiYang2000/RSL (public graph datasets).

## 4. Adaptive Concept Bottleneck for Foundation Models Under Distribution Shifts — CONDA (Choi, Raghuram, Li, Jha; arXiv 2412.14097, Dec 2024)

**Paper.** A CBM on a frozen foundation model: concept scores v = Cφ(x) (m unit-norm concept vectors), label predictor Wv + b (Eq. 1, p.4). Sec. 2.2–2.3 (p.4–5) formalise two shift types — low-level (concept-score distribution unchanged) and concept-level (concept scores change, prediction distribution should not) — and three failure modes: non-robust concept mapping, non-robust linear head, incomplete concept set. CONDA (Sec. 3, p.5–9) is source-free test-time adaptation on unlabeled target batches with pseudo-labels from an ensemble of the FM's zero-shot and linear-probe predictors (take the more confident): CSA adapts C by minimising log(D_intra/D_inter) of Mahalanobis distances to the source class-conditional Gaussians of concept scores (needs only source μ_y, Σ_y) + Frobenius anchor (Eq. 8–9); LPA adapts (W, b) with CE on pseudo-labels + elastic net (Eq. 11); RCB adds r = 5 residual concept vectors and a residual head trained with CE + cosine-diversity + top-k coherency regularisers (Eq. 12–14), auto-captioned via a CLIP-Dissect/ConceptNet procedure (App. B.2). Results (Table 1, p.11): Waterbirds target AVG 32.03 → 60.69 (PCBM), Metashift WG 73.89 → 92.02, CIFAR10-C AVG 82.61 → 84.38; "up to 28%". Fig. 3 (p.12): CSA drives gains under low-level shift, LPA/RCB under concept-level shift (CSA alone can beat all three combined on CIFAR-C). RCB on Waterbirds discovers "feathers, wings, beak" (Sec. 4.4, p.13). Pseudo-label quality is the bottleneck: refined kNN pseudo-labels raise CIFAR100-C 53.88 → 61.25; perfect labels 97.31 (Table 5, p.28); with severity-5 noise where ZS/LP are poor, adaptation *hurts* (Table 8, p.32). Runtime seconds per batch (Table 3, p.23).

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 4.1 — Failure taxonomy as a concept bottleneck for step classification, with residual concepts for unlisted failure modes and CONDA-style adaptation across frameworks.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Eq. 1 (p.4) — predictions are an affine function of concept scores; RCB (Sec. 3.3, p.9) adds r residual concepts with diversity and coherency regularisers and reports them as new human-readable concepts; LPA re-weights concept-to-class mappings under concept-level shift (Fig. 4, p.12: land concepts start to support "waterbird" after adaptation).
- *Mapping:* sample = step embedding φ(s_t) (text encoder or agent-LLM hidden state); concept bank C = embeddings of step-level descriptions of the taxonomy modes (MAST's 14, AgentRx's 9, AgentErrorBench module×type), Oikarinen-style (text embeddings) or CAVs learned from labelled steps; head predicts {no error, mode_1 … mode_K}. This yields "what" and "when" (first step whose non-null mass exceeds a threshold) with an interpretable concept-score vector per step. Across frameworks (train on Who&When/MAST-Data/HORIZON → test on SWE-Bench Pro/τ²-bench/CLI coding), run CONDA: CSA fixes surface/format shift of concept scores; LPA fixes the different mode mix of the new benchmark; RCB surfaces modes the taxonomy lacks (auto-captioned by nearest taxonomy text or an LLM). Pseudo-labels for the target = ensemble of a prompted LLM judge (the "zero-shot" predictor) and the source linear probe. Supervision: L2 on source, L0 on target; text-only.
- *Datasets / baseline:* source Who&When Pro (12k, mode label), MAST-Data, HORIZON, Aegis (agent, mode); target AgentErrorBench (module+type), AgentRx (9 categories), TrajErrBench; baselines = zero-shot LLM judge with the taxonomy in the prompt, linear probe on φ without bottleneck, un-adapted CBM, LPA-only.
- *Relation:* new — neither proposal addresses "what" or interpretability; complementary to both (the concept-score vector is a natural feature for the hazard model and for proposal 1's surrogate f(prefix, step, δ)).
- *Main risk:* MAST-Data/HORIZON/Aegis mode labels are trace-level, not step-level, so step-level concept training needs Who&When Pro or weak labelling; CONDA's gains depend on decent pseudo-labels (Table 8 negative result) and LLM judges are ~10–30% step-accurate on natural failures, which is the regime where the paper reports adaptation hurting.
- *Plausibility 3, Effort M.*

**Card 4.2 — Diagnose the cross-benchmark gap as low-level vs concept-level vs incomplete-concept shift before adapting.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Sec. 2.2–2.3 (p.4–5) definitions (Eq. 2–4) and the component ablation in Fig. 3/Fig. 5a–b (p.12, p.26): if the concept-score distribution P_con shifts but a re-weighted head recovers performance it is low-level; if P_con is stable but the head fails it is concept-level; if nothing recovers, the concept set is incomplete.
- *Mapping:* compute per-mode concept-score distributions of steps on source vs target datasets; compare (i) their divergence, (ii) accuracy after re-fitting only the head on a few target labels, (iii) residual gain from new concepts. This tells whether the large in-domain/cross-domain attribution gaps reported in the field are formatting (fixable by CSA-style alignment or by normalising logs), a different failure-mode mix (fixable by re-weighting with a few labels), or genuinely new failure modes (needs new taxonomy entries). Supervision L2 (small) ; text-only.
- *Datasets / baseline:* any source→target pair among Who&When, TrajErrBench, AgentErrorBench, LongRCA; baseline = reporting the gap without decomposition.
- *Relation:* new, diagnostic; informs where to spend labelling in Cards 2.2 and 4.1.

## 5. DaWin: Training-free Dynamic Weight Interpolation for Robust Adaptation (Oh, Li, Song, Yun, Han; ICLR 2025, arXiv 2410.03782v4)

**Paper.** Two models with the same architecture, θ_0 (zero-shot CLIP) and θ_1 (fine-tuned on ImageNet). Pilot (Table 1, p.3): with ground-truth labels, per-sample interpolation coefficients λ*(x) = exp(−CE_1)/(exp(−CE_0)+exp(−CE_1)) give 60.6 avg OOD accuracy vs 51.0 for static WiSE-FT and 55.0 for per-domain oracle coefficients. DaWin (Eq. 1, p.5) replaces cross-entropy by the prediction entropy H: λ(x) = exp(−H(f(x;θ_1)))/(exp(−H(f(x;θ_0)))+exp(−H(f(x;θ_1)))), after temperature scaling on ID validation and an optional per-domain offset (Eq. 2, App. A.1). Entropy ratio correlates strongly with cross-entropy ratio on all splits except where both models are wrong (Fig. 2, p.4). To avoid N separate merged models, λ(x) are clustered by a Beta mixture (EM, K = 2–5) and each cluster uses one interpolated model (Sec. 3.3, p.6). Results: CLIP ViT-B/32 OOD avg 54.4 vs WiSE-FT 51.0 (ID 78.7 vs 79.1, Table 2, p.7); ViT-B/16 66.9 vs 64.2 (Table 3); ViT-L/14 76.0 vs 73.2 (Table 4). Entropy-based dynamic classifier selection (52.9) and output ensembling (52.7) also beat static merging but less than weight interpolation (Table 5, p.8). Multi-task merging of 8 experts: 86.3 vs AdaMerging++ 80.9, Pareto Merging 84.8, oracle expert selection 90.5 (Table 6, p.9); coefficients concentrate on the true expert and on related tasks (Fig. 7). Lemma 6.1 (p.10): if correct models have lower entropy, their coefficient is ≥ 1/M. Table G (p.28): adding non-expert models hurts Task Arithmetic (87.5 → 81.5) but helps DaWin (90.5 → 94.8). Cost: ~2× FLOPs of a single model, less wall-clock than a WiSE-FT grid search (Table F, p.28).

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 5.1 — Entropy-gated per-trajectory combination of attributor experts trained on different benchmarks.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Eq. 1 (p.5), Lemma 6.1 (p.10), Table 5 (p.8: selection vs output-ensemble vs weight-interpolation), Table 6/Table G (merging many experts of unknown relevance). Expertise is read off the model's own output entropy on the unlabeled test sample; no training, no router.

Fine-tune one attributor (LoRA on the same base LLM, or a hazard head on the same backbone) per dataset = "experts", keep the un-tuned base as θ_0. For a new trajectory from an unknown framework, each expert outputs a distribution over candidate steps (and agents); compute λ_j ∝ exp(−H_j) and either (a) ensemble the step distributions (DOE), (b) select the lowest-entropy expert (DCS), or (c) interpolate LoRA weights per cluster of trajectories (Beta/Dirichlet mixture over λ). Temperature-scale each expert on its own validation split first. Output: when/who distribution; supervision L0 at test; text-only or grey-box. Also applies to interpolating a base judge with a judge fine-tuned on 184 examples (WiSE-FT analogue) to limit over-fitting — the per-sample variant beats the static one by 3–4 points in the paper.
- *Datasets / baseline:* leave-one-dataset-out across Who&When / TrajErrBench / AgentErrorBench / LongRCA / MP-Bench; baselines = single best expert, uniform output average, static WiSE-FT with grid-searched λ, oracle expert selection (upper bound).
- *Relation:* new (model combination is in neither proposal); it is the cheapest way to turn several in-domain attributors into one cross-domain attributor.
- *Main risk:* entropy of a distribution over T = 10–150 candidate steps is confounded by trajectory length and by LLM over-confidence; Lemma 6.1 needs the correct expert to be the least uncertain, which fails in the "FalseFalse" regime that is common here (best models are ~10–30% step-accurate). Weight interpolation requires identical architectures (fine for LoRA experts on one base).

## 6. Understanding Multimodal LLMs Under Distribution Shifts: An Information-Theoretic Approach (Oh, Fang, Im, Du, Li; ICML 2025, arXiv 2502.00577v2)

**Paper.** Defines effective mutual information EMI(P;θ) = I(P_X ⊗ P_θ) − I(P_XY): MI between query and *model* response minus MI between query and *reference* response (Def. 4.2, Eq. 7, p.4), to remove the domain-dependent scale of raw MI (Fig. 5, p.15). Lemma 4.3 / Theorem 4.4 (p.5): |EMI − PM| ≤ δ + 4.4δ^{1/8}, where PM is the logit Bradley-Terry preference with reward r = log P_{Y|X} — i.e. EMI approximates an LLM-judge relative-preference score. EMID = EMI_ID − EMI_OOD; Theorem 4.5 (Eq. 10, p.6; conditionals consistent): EMID ≤ Ĥ(√JS(P_Xv‖Q_Xv) + √JS(P_Xt‖Q_Xt)) + 8Δ^{1/4}, Ĥ = max_x[H(Q_{Y|x}) + H(P_θ(·|x))], Δ = JS gaps between model-output and reference-output marginals; Theorem 4.6 adds conditional-shift terms. Estimation (App. B, p.15–16): CLUB MI estimator (two 2-layer MLPs, hidden 250, trained once on the union of all shift variants, 5,000 iters) on CLIP ViT-B/32 image + XLM-RoBERTa-base text embeddings; RJSD for JS divergence; Ĥ ≈ 4. Validation on 61 shifts (34 synthetic, 27 natural) of LLaVA-Bench COCO with LLaVA-1.5/NeXT 7B/13B, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5: Spearman(EMI, GPT-4 RP score) 0.79/0.65/0.74/0.73 synthetic, 0.46–0.72 natural (Table 2, p.7); Pearson(EMID, bound) 0.746 synthetic, 0.565 natural (Table 3, p.7); on LLaVA-Med 0.93 (Table 5, p.8). Input-only partial bound still moderately predictive (Fig. 4, p.8). EMI is 138× faster than the GPT-4o judge per instance (Table 10, p.20). Using the bound as a regulariser during instruction tuning gives J-shift 59.6 → 60.8 (Table 6, p.9).

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 6.1 — Judge-free "effective relevance" of an action to its context as a per-step signal.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* EMI (Eq. 7) estimated by CLUB: log q_ψ(z_y | z_x) − (1/N)Σ_j log q_ψ(z_yj | z_xi) on frozen embeddings (Eq. 12, p.15); the reference MI I(P_XY) is estimated from (query, reference answer) pairs; Theorem 4.4 ties EMI to preference scores; Table 10 shows the cost advantage.
- *Mapping:* query X = the step's context (previous observation / tool result / page text, plus the instruction), response Y = the agent's action/message at that step. Train the CLUB conditional q_ψ once on (context, action) pairs from *successful* trajectories (the reference distribution, L1); the per-step score is the pointwise log-ratio log q_ψ(z_a | z_c) − log q_ψ(z_a) for steps of failed trajectories, i.e. "how much does this action depend on what the agent just observed". Hallucinated actions, ignored tool errors and repeated loops should score low. Output: ranked candidate steps, a hazard-model feature; text-only, cheap (embedding model + MLP), no LLM judge. Reference-based variant: where a successful sibling run exists (AgentHallu pairs, FaaP), subtract the sibling's relevance at the aligned step to get an EMI-like "effective" value.
- *Datasets / baseline:* AgentHallu (hallucination = action weakly conditioned on observation), SearchAuditBench (1,243 runs, step + root cause), TraceElephant (full LLM-call inputs available); baselines = per-step LLM-judge score, action perplexity under the base model (grey-box), position prior.
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 2 (an L1-trainable per-step feature) and gives proposal 1 a cheap surrogate signal to decide where to intervene.
- *Main risk:* the paper validates EMI only at the *dataset* level (rank correlation across 61 shift datasets), never per sample; frozen-embedding MI captures topical relevance, and many agent errors are topically relevant but wrong (wrong argument value, off-by-one).
- *Plausibility 3, Effort M.*

**Card 6.2 — JS-divergence bound as a predictor of cross-benchmark attribution gaps and as a regulariser.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Theorem 4.5 (Eq. 10, p.6) and the partial-bound experiment (Fig. 4, p.8): √JS of the two input modalities, estimated by RJSD on embeddings with no labels, correlates with the measured performance drop; Eq. 11 (p.8) uses the bound as a training regulariser (shrink representations toward an isotropic Gaussian, scaled by batch output entropy).
- *Mapping:* treat an attributor trained on dataset P and applied to dataset Q; the two "modalities" are the agent-output stream and the observation/tool stream. Compute RJSD(P_actions‖Q_actions) + RJSD(P_obs‖Q_obs) on step embeddings for every pair of the 12+ datasets (no labels, L0); correlate with measured in-domain→cross-domain step-accuracy drops; use it to choose source mixtures and to flag benchmarks that are "far" (e.g., CLI coding vs GAIA web). When fine-tuning an attributor, add Eq. 11's regulariser to its hidden states.
- *Datasets / baseline:* all pairs among Who&When, TrajErrBench, AgentErrorBench, LongRCA, Failure-as-a-Process, SearchAuditBench; baseline = measured gaps only.
- *Relation:* new (meta-level), supports planning for both proposals' cross-dataset evaluations.
- *Main risk:* bound is loose (Pearson 0.565 on natural shifts) and the simplified theorem assumes unchanged conditionals, whereas agent benchmarks differ jointly in task, tools and failure modes (Theorem 4.6 regime, not validated).
- *Plausibility 3, Effort S.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "EMI estimates exhibit a strong correlation with RP score" — Spearman ρ 0.794/0.652/0.738/0.726 (synthetic), 0.610/0.720/0.593/0.457 (natural) (Table 2, p.7).
2. Pearson r between EMID and its upper bound: 0.746 (synthetic), 0.565 (natural), all models (Table 3, p.7); 0.930 on LLaVA-Med (Table 5, p.8).
3. "EMI can shorten the time by 138 times compared to the RP score. Even including the time required for MI training, EMI-based evaluation is still 3 times faster" (App. C.4, p.19); 14.56 s vs 2008.45 s on 90 samples (Table 10).

## 7. Visual Instruction Bottleneck Tuning — Vittle (Oh, Li, Im, Li; NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2505.13946v2)

**Paper.** Adds an information-bottleneck layer inside an MLLM during instruction tuning. Variational lower bound of IB tailored to the autoregressive multimodal case (Eq. 2–4, p.4): I(Z,X) ≤ E[KL(p(z_v|x_v)‖r(z_v))] + E[KL(p(z_t|x_v,x_t)‖r(z_t))], I(Z,Y) ≥ E[log q(y|z)]. Implementation (Sec. 3.3, Fig. 2, p.5): at layer l = 24 of 32 (Vicuna-7B), two per-token MLPs output Gaussian posterior (μ, σ²); sample z̃ with reparameterisation; feed ẑ = (1−α)z + αz̃ with α cosine-annealed to 0.5 (α > 0.5 diverges, Table 11, p.26); at inference ẑ = (z+μ)/2; β = 0.1/d; prior fixed N(0,I) (Vittle(F)) or learnable (Vittle(L)). Proposition 3.2 (p.6): EMID ≤ Ĥ(√JS(P_Zv‖Q_Zv) + √JS(P_Zt‖Q_Zt) + √Δ_X|Z) + |H(P_Yθ)−H(P_Y)| + |H(Q_Yθ)−H(Q_Y)| — the bound is on *representation* divergence, so compressing Z toward a shared prior lowers it. Results (LLaVA-1.5-7B, 45 datasets, 30 shifts): LB-COCO joint perturbation RP 62.3 → 64.4, text pert. 72.2 → 74.1 (Table 3/12, p.8, p.28); POPE visual shifts 84.12 → 84.99; long-tail LB-Wilder 156.9 → 168.8 (L) (Table 1, p.8); general benchmarks unchanged (50.9 vs 50.9/51.1, Table 2); JSD between clean and perturbed layer-24 representations 0.068 → 0.047, EMID 0.026 → 0.021 (Table 4, p.9). Weight-space regularisers fail (LoRA joint-pert 39.7, weight decay 59.5) and information-maximising objectives (ROSS, LIT) are worse on open-ended QA (Fig. 6, Table 12). +1.5% params, +20% train time, inference cost unchanged (Table 5). Early-layer bottlenecks (8–20) do not converge (App. B.1). Limitation: slightly hurts OCR.

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 7.1 — IB bottleneck when fine-tuning a step attributor/hazard head for cross-framework robustness.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Eq. 5 (p.4) objective; Sec. 3.3 architecture (bottleneck at 75% depth, separate posteriors per input stream, interpolation gate); Proposition 3.2's representation-space EMID bound; Table 12's comparison showing LoRA/weight decay are the wrong regularisers for shift robustness.
- *Mapping:* when training an open LLM (or a head on its hidden states) to output h_t / the decisive step from labelled trajectories of several frameworks, insert a Vittle layer with two posteriors: one over observation/tool-output tokens, one over agent-output tokens, KL to N(0,I) with β = 0.1/d, bottleneck at ~75% depth. The intent is to discard framework-specific surface (tool-call syntax, log formatting, verbosity) and keep failure-relevant content, shrinking the in-domain/cross-domain gap. Supervision L2 (step labels) or L1 (outcome-only hazard model); grey-box (we train the model). Output: when (and P(fail|prefix) if the head is a hazard).
- *Datasets / baseline:* train on Who&When + TrajErrBench + AgentErrorBench (+ Who&When Pro), test on LongRCA / MP-Bench / Failure-as-a-Process; baselines = plain SFT, LoRA, weight decay, WiSE-FT interpolation (Card 5.1), observation-text normalisation (a data-side alternative).
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 2 (robust hazard model) and the learned surrogate of proposal 1.
- *Main risk:* measured gains are 1–3 points and larger for perturbations than for long-tail shifts; agent-benchmark shift is long-tail/joint, not cosmetic. Full fine-tuning of a 7B model is required (LoRA was the worst baseline). The OCR degradation warns that compression can discard exact tokens (file paths, argument values) that attribution needs.
- *Plausibility 3, Effort L.*

**Card 7.2 — Surface-perturbation invariance test for attributors.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Fig. 1(b) and Fig. 8/13 (p.2, p.9, p.27): semantically equivalent clean and perturbed queries land far apart in layer-24 representations of LLaVA, and responses change (Fig. 7, p.9); the paper measures pairwise cosine distance and JSD between clean and perturbed sets as a robustness metric (Table 4).
- *Mapping:* build perturbed twins of each trajectory that preserve semantics — rename tools, re-serialise JSON, change log formatting, paraphrase agent messages, translate messages — and measure (i) agreement of the attributor's t*/agent/category between twin and original, (ii) representation distance of the judge's hidden states if grey-box. Report invariance alongside accuracy; use the twins as augmentation when fine-tuning. Supervision: none beyond existing labels (L2 for agreement with ground truth, L0 for self-consistency); text-only.
- *Datasets / baseline:* Who&When, TrajErrBench, AgentErrorBench; baseline = current single-format evaluation.
- *Relation:* new evaluation protocol; feeds Card 7.1 and Card 4.2.
- *Main risk:* some "surface" changes alter failure semantics (renaming a tool can mask a tool-misuse error); perturbation design must be checked by hand.
- *Plausibility 4, Effort S.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "Vittle consistently improves the MLLM's robustness under shifts" across "45 datasets, including 30 shift scenarios" (Abstract); LB-COCO joint perturbation 62.3 → 64.4, text 72.2 → 74.1 (Table 3, p.8).
2. Table 4 (p.9): JSD 0.068 → 0.047 and EMID 0.026 → 0.021 between clean and perturbed representations.
3. "explicit regularization on weight-space does not ensure a good balance between adaptability on in-distribution and robustness to distribution shifts" — LoRA joint-pert 39.7, WD 59.5 vs baseline 62.3 (Table 3, p.8).
4. "using too large values of α results in diverging language modeling loss, indicating that using a strongly penalized representation only cannot predict proper response tokens" (App. B.1, p.26; α ≥ 0.75 failed to converge).
5. "Although Vittle increases the training time up to 20% compared with baseline, its inference time is almost identical to the original model" (Sec. 4.2, p.10); +1.5% parameters.

## 8. Unsupervised Domain Adaptation for Binary Classification with an Unobservable Source Subpopulation (Ying, Jin, Zhang, Tian, Ma, Li, Zhao; TMLR 04/2026, arXiv 2509.20587v3)

**Paper.** Binary label Y, binary background A, domain R (1 = labelled source, 0 = unlabelled target with A observed). Structured missingness: pr(Y=1, A=1 | R=1) = 0 (Eq. 2, p.5) — one (label, background) cell is absent from the source. Assumption (Eq. 3, p.5): p(X | Y, A) is identical across domains (label shift conditional on background). Proposition 1 (Eq. 7, p.6) recovers the target posteriors without ever observing the missing cell: η_1(x) = 1 − (β_01/α_01)·((1−π)/π)·κ(x)/(1−κ(x)), where κ(x) = pr(R=1 | x, A=1) is a *source-vs-target discriminator* trained on A=1 samples only; η_0(x) = (β_10/α_10)ξ_0(x) / [(β_10/α_10)ξ_0(x) + (β_00/α_00)(1−ξ_0(x))] re-weights the source classifier ξ_0; η(x) mixes them with τ_0(x) = pr(A=1 | x, R=0). Only the target cell proportions β need estimating. Sec. 4.3 (p.7): identifiability is the PU-learning anchor-set condition (a region of X where the negative class has mass and the positive class none), framed as a special case of open-set label shift. Lemma 2 (Eq. 11, p.8): KL distribution matching reduces to maximising E[log(ξ_0(X) b_1^{-1} β_10 + (1−ξ_0(X))(1−b_1)^{-1}(ϱ − β_10)) | R=0, A=0] — a one-dimensional concave problem needing only the source classifier, not densities. Theorem 1 (p.9): ‖β̂ − β‖_1 = O(r_n √log n + n^{-1/2}); Proposition 2: excess target risk of re-weighted ERM bounded by Rademacher complexity + ‖β̂ − β‖_1 + O(n^{-1/2}). Experiments: 4-d Gaussian synthetic (Fig. 1, p.11) and Waterbirds with frozen ResNet-18/ViT-16 embeddings + L2 logistic regression (Table 3, Fig. 2–4, p.11–13): proposed η beats Naive1 (apply source model) and Naive2 (plain label shift) in accuracy/F1 across most settings, best when the informative cell (Y=0, A=1) is well represented (a = 0.7); degrades when another cell is very small; ViT features help all methods.

**Transferable ideas.**

**Card 8.1 — Closed-form error-step posterior from a success-vs-failure step discriminator plus a mixture proportion (PU view of failed trajectories).**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* Proposition 1 (Eq. 7, p.6): the posterior of the never-observed positive cell equals 1 − (ratio of cell proportions) × odds of a domain discriminator κ(x); Sec. 4.3 (p.7): "This is a canonical positive-unlabeled (PU) learning problem … recovering pr(Y=1, A=1) from a mixture of pr(Y=1, A=1) and pr(Y=0, A=1), given direct access to pr(Y=0, A=1)"; Lemma 2 (p.8) estimates the proportion by KL matching using only a classifier.
- *Mapping (direct):* Y = step is erroneous; the "background" A = trajectory outcome; R indexes "pure" vs "mixed" populations. Steps from successful trajectories are the observed cell (Y=0, A=success); the cell (Y=1, A=success) is empty by construction (nearly: unrecovered errors do not occur in successes); steps from failed trajectories are the mixture. Then: (i) train κ(x) = P(step comes from a *failed* trajectory | step embedding) on all steps using only outcome labels (L1); (ii) estimate the error-step proportion β_err by Lemma-2-style KL matching, or by any mixture-proportion estimator (Ramaswamy et al. 2016; the paper's Eq. 10) with the success steps as the known component; (iii) P(step t is an error | failed run) = 1 − (β_good/α_good)·(n_succ/n_fail)·κ(x_t)/(1−κ(x_t)) — a per-step error probability without any step labels. Output: ranked steps, error set (threshold), decisive step (earliest above threshold), and a dataset-level prior "how many steps per failed run are wrong" (useful for set-F1 and for the hazard prior). Text-only (embeddings) or grey-box (hidden states). The anchor-set condition has a natural reading: there must exist steps that are always good (e.g., initial planning/boilerplate steps) — testable.
- *Datasets / baseline:* AgentHallu (250 clean vs 443 hallucinated runs, step labels for validation), Failure-as-a-Process (610 PASS / 1,184 FAIL, t_err/t_lock/t_obs), HORIZON successes + TRAIL, Who&When Pro (injected; exact labels) as a sanity check; baselines = proposal 2's L1 hazard model, OAT one-class, LLM judge, Naive "apply success-vs-fail classifier odds directly without proportion correction" (= the paper's Naive2).
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 2 substantially — it supplies an estimator with identifiability conditions and finite-sample bounds for exactly the L1 setting, and a principled way to estimate the mixture proportion the hazard model otherwise fits implicitly; the KL-matching proportion estimate also calibrates P(fail | prefix).
- *Main risk:* the invariance assumption p(X | Y, A) says good steps in failed runs are distributed like good steps in successful runs; post-error steps (propagation, recovery attempts) violate it, as do trajectory-level confounders (hard tasks fail more and look different) — κ(x) may learn task difficulty rather than step quality. Mitigation: condition κ on task/prefix features or restrict to pre-t_obs steps; the paper itself warns performance "can degrade, even falling behind the two incorrect naive benchmarks" when a cell is very small (p.13).
- *Plausibility 4, Effort S/M.*

**Card 8.2 — Role-conditioned mixture proportions for "who" without step labels.**
- *Mechanism in the paper:* the background variable A is observed in the target and conditions everything (κ trained on A=1 only; η_0 re-weighted by β_·0/α_·0 per cell); Discussion (p.14) notes extension to multi-level A at the cost of (n_y − 2)n_a + 1 anchor assumptions, "as long as the label is binary … one anchor set assumption is sufficient if only one subpopulation is missing".
- *Mapping:* in multi-agent logs let A = agent role (orchestrator / web-surfer / coder / verifier). Per role, estimate the error-step proportion β_err,role by distribution matching between that role's steps in failed vs successful runs (L1 only). Rank roles by β_err,role × (number of steps) to get a dataset-level responsibility prior and, per failed run, combine with Card 8.1's per-step posterior to produce "who". Binary Y keeps identifiability to a single anchor assumption per role.
- *Datasets / baseline:* Who&When (role + step labels for validation), MP-Bench (three annotators, set-valued), Aegis (agent-level labels, 9.5k, no step); baseline = uniform role prior, LLM judge agent accuracy (~55%).
- *Relation:* strengthens proposal 2 ("who" is a summary statistic in both proposals but neither gives an L1 estimator of per-role error rates).
- *Main risk:* roles have very different step counts and content distributions; a role that only acts after errors (verifier) will have its steps mislabelled as off-manifold.
- *Plausibility 3, Effort S.*

**Quotable facts.**
1. "Naively ignoring this unobserved group can result in biased estimates and degraded predictive performance. Despite this structured missingness, we show that the prediction in the target domain can still be recovered." (Abstract).
2. Eq. 7 (p.6): η_1(x) = 1 − (β_01/α_01)·((1−π)/π)·κ(x)/(1−κ(x)) with κ(x) = pr(R=1 | x, A=1) — the never-observed cell's posterior from a domain discriminator.
3. "This is a canonical positive-unlabeled (PU) learning problem. Identifiability in this setting is governed by the standard anchor set condition" (Sec. 4.3, p.7).
4. "A key advantage of minimizing the KL divergence is that it circumvents the need to explicitly estimate the generative probabilities p_10(x) and p_00(x) … it suffices to estimate one background-specific prediction probability ξ_0(x)" (Sec. 4.4, p.8).
5. "the performance of the proposed η(x) can degrade, even falling behind the two incorrect naive benchmarks, if the proportion of either (Y=1, A=0) or (Y=0, A=0) becomes exceedingly small" (Sec. 7, p.13).

1. **Card 8.1 — PU/mixture-proportion estimator: P(step is error) from a success-vs-failure step discriminator corrected by a KL-matched error proportion (Ying et al.).** Needs only outcome labels, has identifiability conditions and bounds, and is a drop-in strengthening of proposal 2. Plausibility 4, Effort S/M.
2. **Card 2.1 — Gradient-projection acquisition of steps to label or roll out (Bai et al.).** Turns the expensive perturbation screen of proposal 1 and the step-label scarcity of proposal 2 into a budgeted active-learning problem; the paper shows ~1,000 labels saturate and the score beats entropy/margin/random. Plausibility 4, Effort M.
3. **Card 3.1 — Feature-resonance score (one-step representation movement of failed-run steps while a linear layer is fit to success-run steps) as a label-free candidate ranker (Yang et al.).** Cheapest L1 signal in the batch, no taxonomy needed, computable per dataset so no cross-domain transfer is required. Plausibility 4, Effort M.
4. **Card 1.2 + 7.2 — Evaluation protocol additions: dual/abstention accuracy on clean runs and surface-perturbation invariance of attributors (survey; Vittle).** Small effort, exposes two likely weaknesses of prompted judges (never abstain; format-sensitive). Plausibility 4, Effort S.
5. **Card 4.1 — Failure taxonomy as a concept bottleneck with residual concepts and CONDA-style source-free adaptation across frameworks (Choi et al.).** Only idea in the batch that targets "what" and interpretability together with the cross-benchmark gap; limited by pseudo-label quality. Plausibility 3, Effort M.

Runners-up: Card 5.1 (entropy-gated combination of per-benchmark attributor experts, plausibility 3, S/M); Card 6.1 (judge-free effective relevance per step, 3, M); Card 7.1 (IB bottleneck in a fine-tuned attributor, 3, L); Card 6.2 (JSD bound to predict cross-benchmark gaps, 3, S).
